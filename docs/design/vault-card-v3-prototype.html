vault card · v3 · first principles

A card is a moment in a journey, not a row in a table.

The card stops pretending to be a Linear ticket. The journey through skills becomes the primary visual element. Type does the work that chips were doing. Confidence and decay are visible, not hidden behind crisp UI. The system narrates itself.

I · The journey is the protagonist

Every vault item passes through a sequence of skills. The card shows that sequence — where it's been, where it is, where it's going — as a small constellation of dots. The kanban column tells you the stage; the journey strip tells you the story.

III · Failure is felt, not styled

A failed card doesn't get a red FAILED chip in the corner. The whole card becomes visually distressed — a subtle diagonal texture that reads, peripherally, as wrong. The journey shows the broken step. The narration explains what happened, in prose. The single gesture offered is the one you'd want: retry.

IV · The system admits uncertainty

When the classifier was confident, the card has crisp edges. When it wasn't, the card looks softer — almost watercoloured. The title shifts to italic, hinting "I'm not sure about this one." This is rare in software: most UIs project confidence even when the data is shaky. Here it's honest. Low confidence means look closer.

V · Old things look old

Most kanban tools hide age behind a number. A 23-day-old card looks identical to a fresh one until you read the corner. Here, age desaturates the card — it visibly fades. You feel the staleness peripherally; the column starts to look tired in places. That's a signal to clean up, not a number to ignore.

VI · No chrome, only content

No project chips, no priority chips, no type chips, no tag clouds, no button rows. Project becomes a tint and a foot-margin signature. Priority becomes the warmth of the card. Type becomes implicit in the journey (references have a short journey; tasks a longer one). Tags are gone — they live on the detail page, where there's room to take them seriously. The card is a poster, not a form.

The risk of this design is honest: it's bigger. Each card takes more vertical space than a Linear-style row, so fewer fit on screen at once. That's a trade I'd argue is correct for your system — most of your time on this board is observation, not triage. You don't need to see 30 items; you need to read the four or five that matter right now. A card you read once and act on is worth more than a row you skim and forget.

If density turns out to be load-bearing somewhere, the journey strip + narration are the two pieces I'd keep last; everything else is negotiable.
